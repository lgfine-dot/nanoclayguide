This pillar covers the two halves of getting nanoclay into your process with confidence: buying the right material, and proving you got it.

## Buying it

[How to Buy Nanoclay](/blog/how-to-buy-nanoclay-sourcing-guide/) starts where you should — deciding what type you actually need — then covers who the major suppliers are, what to put in a request for quotation, the minimum order quantities to expect at each stage, and the sourcing mistakes that recur.

[How to Specify Nanoclay](/blog/how-to-specify-nanoclay-buyers-checklist/) turns that into a written specification: mineral and modifier, purity and composition, physical form, the process-fit parameters people forget, and the documentation to demand. It ends with a worked example.

[Nanoclay Supplier Comparison](/blog/nanoclay-supplier-comparison/) maps the consolidated supply base — BYK, Nanocor, Southern Clay, the halloysite specialists and the regional commodity producers — and how to choose between them.

## Proving it

Four techniques, each answering a different question, and none sufficient alone.

[XRD](/blog/xrd-nanoclay-characterization-d-spacing/) reads d-spacing, which is the closest thing to a direct measurement of whether the layers have opened — including the interpretation trap that makes a good pattern look better than it is. [TEM and SEM imaging](/blog/tem-sem-imaging-nanoclays-dispersion/) shows what good dispersion actually looks like, and explains why microscopy and diffraction have to be read together.

[FTIR](/blog/ftir-analysis-organoclays-surface-modification/) is the fast, accessible check that a clay really was organically modified. [Rheology testing](/blog/rheology-testing-nanoclay-dispersions/) doubles as a performance measurement and a sensitive probe of dispersion quality — shear-thinning, yield stress and thixotropy, and how to use them as a screening tool.
